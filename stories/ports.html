Brazil's ports hit a record. Suape grew in value, not in weight.

Five years of every berthing in the country, rebuilt from the regulator's raw files. The national total went up, Suape's tonnage went sideways, and the reason its ships wait less than the neighbours' is hiding in the cargo mix.

Source ANTAQ open dataPeriod 2021–2025Tools Python · DuckDB · Power BIGap to official total ≤ 1.16%

A record year, carried by more, smaller ships

Brazilian ports handled 1,403.8 million tonnes in 2025, up 5.9% on 2024 and the highest in the series. The number of berthings grew faster than the cargo: 94,118 in 2025 against 66,484 in 2021. Each call now moves less, about 14.9 thousand tonnes per berthing, down from 18.2 thousand. More calls with less cargo each means more pressure on berth scheduling, even when the tonnage looks healthy.

ANTAQ's 2025 yearbook reports growth of 6.1%, not 5.9%. My 2025 total matches the yearbook (+0.05%), but my 2024 base is 0.26% higher because ANTAQ revised the 2024 data after closing the yearbook. I kept the number the data gives and documented the gap instead of forcing it to match.

Suape traded tonnes for containers

Suape, the main port in Pernambuco, moved 24.3 million tonnes in 2025, 2.3% less than in 2024. It ranks 13th among all Brazilian facilities, with 1.7% of the national total. On weight alone it looks stuck.

Containers tell another story. Suape handled 698 thousand TEU in 2025 against 519 thousand in 2021, 34.6% more, and containers rose from 26.7% to 31.2% of its tonnage. A container carries manufactured goods worth far more per tonne than fuel or grain, so a flat tonnage line with a rising container share points to growth in value.

Ships wait less at Suape than at its neighbours

The median ship waited 4.3 hours to berth at Suape in 2025. The simple average of the medians at the eight other Northeast ports was 26.4 hours, but that average is pulled up by Itaqui, where the typical ship waited almost six days. I use the median because a handful of ships stuck for weeks would otherwise define the whole port.

The gap is not new, but it is narrowing: Suape's median was 1.0 hour in 2021 and reached 5.9 hours in 2024. Month by month in 2025, Suape stayed below the peer average every month; only January came close (17.2 h against 18.1 h).

The cargo mix explains the queue

Split Suape's 2025 waits by type of cargo and the picture changes. Container ships barely wait. Liquid bulk ships, carrying fuel, crude and LPG, wait more than a day.

Anyone trying to shorten Suape's queue should start at the liquid berths, not the container terminal. Rain is the largest single cause of stopped hours, so planning around the rainy season matters more here than in the country as a whole. And the national operating rate is higher mainly because of iron-ore terminals loading at enormous speed, so Suape's lower number is a cargo-mix effect more than a sign of a slow port.

How I built it

  • Data: ANTAQ's raw files for every berthing, cargo record, time record and stoppage, 2021–2025, plus Comex Stat product codes and the IBGE map of Brazil.
  • Pipeline: Python and DuckDB apply ANTAQ's official counting rule, clean impossible times and export a star schema (three fact tables, six dimensions).
  • Checks: pytest compares my yearly totals to ANTAQ's (tolerance ±2%, actual −0.13% to +1.16%) and checks every number quoted in the README against the data.
  • Report: the Power BI file (PBIP) is generated by code, then reviewed in Power BI Desktop with automated screenshots.
